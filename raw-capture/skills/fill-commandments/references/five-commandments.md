## Ownership

The antagonist and the protagonist own different Commandments. **The owner decides which beat a Commandment sits on** — a Commandment labelled onto the wrong side of the exchange is mislabelled even when the sequence of events is correct.

| Commandment | Owner | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Inciting Incident | antagonism | The event that upsets the protagonist's balance. It arrives; he does not cause it. |
| Turning Point Progressive Complication | protagonism | His attempt to hold the status quo together. |
| Crisis | antagonism | The ultimatum. Two options, both costly, both spelled out in the text. |
| Climax | protagonism | His decision — one of the two options in the ultimatum, and nothing else. |
| Resolution | antagonism | What comes of the Climax, and how the terms of the Crisis actually land. |

Because of that ownership, **Crisis (antagonism) → Climax (protagonism) → Resolution (antagonism) is already a valid three-beat event.** If those three are not adjacent or nearly so, the tail of the scene is padded.

**The Crisis must spell the ultimatum out.** Both horns, in the Crisis text itself, in plain terms. This is the one place the register runs long, because the dilemma has to be legible on the page and not only in the analysis. A Crisis stated as "he faces an impossible choice" has done nothing.

**The Climax picks one of the two.** If the Climax is an option that was not in the Crisis, either the Crisis was stated wrong or the scene has a third thing in it that has not been named.

## The Turning Point — one act, two descriptions

**An attempt to return to stasis** and **a deliberate act taken to avoid the Crisis decision** are the same thing viewed from opposite ends. Neither replaces the other.

They reconcile because **the Crisis is the irreversible break from the status quo.** Up to it there still appear to be ways of keeping everything — the Turning Point is him taking one of them. At the Crisis those run out: one of two things has to be given up or changed, and there is no longer a version of the choice in which nothing falls. So an act that maintains is, necessarily, an act that postpones the break. Avoidance and restoration are the same motion described forwards and backwards.

**Note on the Outline.** Pavi's definition (2026-08-25) is narrower than `Bible/06 - Outline.md`, which glosses the slot as *Progressive Complication / Turning Point* — the complication that turns, where new information forces the value to shift. His refinement is that the turn manifests **as an owned act, not as received information**. The two are compatible; his is the operative one, and it is what this skill tests against. The Outline needs a matching edit that only he can make — logged as **O36** in `Bible/99 - Decision Log.md`.

## The intention test — staging decides placement

A Turning Point is an act the protagonist **commits to**. Intention is the qualifier, not chronology: the first thing that happens after the reactive run is not automatically the Turning Point, and an act he merely lets happen is not an act he owns.

This makes staging structural rather than decorative. Where the same beat could be written either way, how it is written decides whether it is the Turning Point.

- **As a decision** — he looks the man in the eye and says no. That carries intention. It is a commitment, and it can be the Turning Point.
- **As a deflection** — non-committal, ambient, neither yes nor no, and the offer simply drifts back out of the room. Nothing was decided, so nothing was owned. The Turning Point is the next act that actually commits.

When two candidate acts compete for the slot, the test is **which one does he actually decide at** — and then the other one gets written so that it does not pretend to be a decision. The prose and the structure have to agree; a beat staged as a shrug cannot carry the Commandment, however well-placed it looks in the outline.

**The first thing he owns is often a refusal** — turning down the help, the offer, or the question that would have resolved the unit — but the refusal is the negative half. The Turning Point is the positive act that follows it, the thing he does to keep going on his own terms.

---

## Two tests — pick by how visible the ultimatum is

**Test A — backward from the Crisis.** Use when the ultimatum is legible early: an action scene, a rescue, anything where the reader can see from the opening that the choice will be safety or danger, this life or that one. Name the Crisis, then find the act that was trying to keep it from arriving. The Turning Point connects forward, directly.

**Test B — backward from the Turning Point to the Inciting Incident.** Use when the scene is social, emotional, or subtle, and neither the reader nor the protagonist can see what the ultimate choice will be until the Crisis lands. Name the status quo the Inciting Incident disrupted, then find the act that tries to restore it. **At the Turning Point not everything is on the table yet, so the Turning Point has to be relevant to everything before it, not to the Crisis.** Reaching forward in a scene of this kind produces something causally tidy that is really only foreshadowing.

The test is chosen by the scene, not by preference. Where both apply they find the same act; where they disagree, the scene type decides which one is being run.

## Worked example — Test B

From Pavi's own pass on KnitRPG Scene 1 (Material), 2026-08-27, kept as calibration rather than as canon.

The status quo is **the inventory, the ability to make the thing, and the certainty that it will work.** The Inciting Incident breaks the certainty: the piece he was most confident in is dead. He keeps trying to get back — the diagnostics, the journal, the swatch. The Turning Point is **frogging the gauntlet**: he can still do it alone, still do it with crochet, still produce a finished piece that works, and he does not have to spend the good skein.

Refusing Amble's yarn belongs to the run of complications around it — the negative half, where the Turning Point is the positive act — and it stays a complication because of **how it is staged**. Cirro never gives a clear no. The exchange is ambient; he neither takes the yarn nor declines it, and Amble carries it back out with something like *let me know if you do want it.* Nothing was decided in that doorway. He decides at the frogging. Written the other way — a flat, eye-contact refusal — the same beat would carry enough intention to be the Turning Point itself, and the frogging would drop back to being its execution. That is the whole point of the intention test: the staging decides it.

Once the refusal is behind him and the frogged sash refuses to tell him anything, going back stops being available. Ship the contaminated piece and pretend he knows, or cut the irreplaceable skein. Either way something in the status quo falls — which is what makes it the Crisis and not another complication.

## Progressive Complications

They **run from the Inciting Incident**, not only after the Turning Point. The shape is:

**II → a run in which the protagonist is acted upon and his options progressively narrow → TP → possibly a second run → Crisis → Climax → Resolution**

**The first run usually carries most of the scene.** Collapsing it makes the protagonist decisive far too early and skips the passage where the scene earns its choice.

Locked as **L57** in `Bible/99 - Decision Log.md`, correcting an earlier version that placed the un-numbered PC run inside the TP → Crisis span alone.

**Still five slots.** PC is not a sixth commandment — it is an un-numbered run, and there are now two possible positions for one rather than one.

**Which run a beat belongs to** is decided by whether it precedes or follows the first act the protagonist owns — not by how escalated it feels. A high-tension beat in which he is still reacting belongs to the first run.
